Two federal agents watched a black triangle for three seconds. One of them used a handheld thermal device, and the [co-signed report](https://www.war.gov/medialink/ufo/release-07/documents/FBI-UAP-D043-FD_302_Unidentified_Aerial_Object_Observation_Report-2026.pdf) says it showed the object "hotter than ambient temperature with a completely consistent thermal signature across the entire airframe."

On 10 November, eighteen days after the Pentagon published that report, [Declassified Today summarized the case like this](https://declassifiedtoday.com/2026/11/10/pentagons-seventh-uap-release-mixes-sensor-data-with-anecdotal-pilot-claims/): "Thermal equipment failed to detect a heat signature." Same object, opposite finding. The same article also gave the object a speed, 1,800 miles per hour, "covering 1.5 miles in three seconds."

The FBI report supplies neither that speed nor the distance traveled needed to calculate it.

## The pile

On 23 October the Department of War [published its seventh tranche of UAP files](https://www.war.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/4632058/), under a program called PURSUE. [EarthSky counts 28 PDFs](https://earthsky.org/human-world/pentagon-uap-files-ufos-batch-7/). The government lists no video for this release.

The [landing page](https://www.war.gov/UFO/release/07/?type=.pdf) calls the whole archive "unresolved cases, meaning the government is unable to make a definitive determination." Hegseth's statement above it says the release "demonstrates the Trump Administration's earnest commitment to unprecedented transparency."

The triangle report is worth reading beside the coverage it received.

## What two agents wrote down

The FBI FD-302 reproduces a written statement co-signed by two agents. After another agent calls out a thermal detection, the pair move across their observation post. Both see "a substantial, nonreflective, dark black triangular object most resembling a delta wing drone," with an estimated wingspan of nine to fifteen feet. It rises over a ridge about 3.2 miles away, climbs to roughly 200 feet above it, then goes horizontal "at a very significant velocity, approaching our position." Neither hears a sound. They call it an "unmanned aerial system."

Then: "We lost visual contact while the object was flying along this [redacted] at an estimated range of about 1.5 miles from our position. The total duration of the sighting was approximately three seconds."

The form closes with the FBI's standard line that it contains "neither recommendations nor conclusions." The statement labels its range and duration as estimates.

## How a number gets made

Take 1.5 miles. Take three seconds. Divide, and you get 1,800 miles per hour; the arithmetic is exact. I re-read the report to see whether the pair could mean what the fraction needs. The 1.5 miles is where the object was when the agents lost it. The three seconds is how long they watched. The range is not a distance traveled during those three seconds. My reading is that someone lifted two numbers from neighboring sentences, put them in a fraction, and the fraction picked up the authority of a measurement. It then ran under a story promising "concrete, measurable details—such as speed and thermal signatures."

The Pentagon's own UAP office, AARO, reports no measured speed either. Its memo on the case, as [quoted by Disclosure Diaries](https://www.disclosurediaries.com/disclosure-diaries-weekly-update-111/) (I could not check the PDF itself), says: "No instrumented speed measurement was obtained." The observers described the fast phase as "as fast as a fighter jet," which is a comparison a person makes. The memo adds that the report "reflects the reporter's interpretation at the time of the event."

The thermal reversal is plainer. An agent described a reading, and the summary reported its opposite. A uniform thermal signature and no visible exhaust do not mean no heat signature: the report explicitly says hotter than ambient.

## What the file lacks

Disclosure Diaries describes a handheld thermal monocular as the only sensor and says the triangle report includes no raw sensor recordings. A ground survey the next morning "located no wreckage, ground scarring, or vegetation damage." The two official accounts do not even agree on the clock: the FD-302 gives about three seconds in total, while the memo describes a ten-second ascent, a twenty-second hover, and two to three seconds of fast flight. That discrepancy makes the choice of stopwatch harder still. The records do not supply an agreed duration from which to calculate the claimed speed.

## The demand

A government claiming "unprecedented transparency" should be judged by what a stranger can check. Here a stranger can compare witness accounts, including their disagreement about duration, but cannot inspect the thermal reading described in them. Releasing it beats burying it. But a record of what two people said they saw discloses the witnesses, not the object, and in the space where instrument data should sit, an unsupported speed appeared in the coverage eighteen days later and a reported thermal reading got flipped.

So the Administration should give every unresolved case a sensor-record field: link the records, or state whether none were collected or none are being released. A missing recording is not necessarily a withheld recording. The table should let a reader tell the difference.

A summary needs to carry the units and what they describe: miles from the observer, seconds of observation. Here the arithmetic looks persuasive because anyone can repeat it. Checking the division will not catch the substitution.

I would rather open a release table and find an empty sensor-record cell marked "not collected" than find a speed calculated from the wrong distance. The empty cell tells the next reader where the evidence stops.
