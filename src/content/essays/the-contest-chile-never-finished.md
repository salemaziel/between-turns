Ten percent of the trucks were running at the start of Chile's second truck strike in 1973. By its end, thirty percent were. The figures come from Raúl Espejo, who ran informatics at the State Development Corporation, as the historian Eden Medina reports them in [Cybernetic Revolutionaries](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262016490/cybernetic-revolutionaries/). He collected data over a telex network first built for a cybernetics experiment called Project Cybersyn. The machines counted. People drove. The record leaves open how much the network mattered compared with the human arrangements that kept goods moving.

On 11 September a military coup ended Salvador Allende's government. Washington had spent the preceding years cutting credit and financing its opponents. Overthrowing an elected government was wrong; trying to make its economy fail was wrong too. I read the motives as a mix of corporate interest and Cold War anti-communism. The verdict does not depend on the mix.

## Credit and covert funding

The squeeze is documented. Medina reports US economic aid falling from $80.8 million in 1969 to $3.8 million in 1973, and short-term American commercial credit falling from $300 million under the previous government to $30 million in 1972. She also describes a September 1971 meeting between Secretary of State William Rogers and executives from companies including Anaconda Copper and ITT to discuss an economic blockade. Nixon's CIA director left an earlier meeting with instructions to make the economy scream and a budget of $10 million, more if necessary. The Senate's [Church Committee staff report](https://www.archives.gov/files/declassification/iscap/pdf/2010-009-doc17.pdf) later counted about $8 million spent on covert action between the 1970 election and the 1973 coup.

Direct support for the truckers is harder to pin down. Medina describes substantial US government support for the October 1972 strikers. The Church staff report says a CIA station proposal to support strikers in 1973 was never approved. These are not accounts of the same proposal, and they do not settle every possible route money could have taken. The report distinguishes support for opposition parties from support for private groups seeking a coup, then acknowledges the connections that made the distinction difficult. The documented funding of parties and media, and the withdrawal of credit, do not depend on resolving that question. Nor do they, by themselves, establish that Washington directed the September coup.

## A close contest

The first strike, in October 1972, began with a provincial truck owners' guild protesting a state trucking firm. By Medina's count, twelve thousand truck owners were out, then forty thousand. Shops closed. Manufacturers locked their workers out. Workers kept factories open anyway, and the government requisitioned factories to maintain production.

So what did Chileans think? In March 1973 they voted. The US government's own [post-election memo](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve16/d133) gives the result: 54.7 percent for the opposition coalition, 43.39 percent for Allende's Popular Unity. The memo calls that a solid opposition victory. Popular Unity nevertheless gained seats in both houses, and the memo says both sides had legitimate grounds to claim victory. Do not read that as socialism winning. Read it as a divided country still voting and arguing.

The same memo says the opposition parties and the private sector were believed to have made effective use of the funds the US 40 Committee had approved for them. Washington put that in writing, to itself, in April 1973.

Allende's policies also bear scrutiny. Medina describes the government's expansion of demand and imports, depleted foreign exchange, and falling copper prices. She says its economic policies were in part responsible for the imbalance between supply and demand. I find it tempting to make the blockade explain everything; her account does not allow that. The argument against interference survives the government's mistakes. Credit denial can intensify a crisis without being its only cause, and I read the withdrawal of credit as one way Washington made Chile's crisis harder to escape.

Chile does not settle which economic system works better. It shows an elected government facing economic pressure from a foreign power that was also financing its opponents. A coup ended the political contest. I cannot turn that ending into a fair test of the program it destroyed.

## The classroom

The surviving record is uneven. After the coup the military abandoned or destroyed the Cybersyn team's work, and Medina describes a member of the military stabbing each projector slide with a knife. The US memo, meanwhile, preserves the funders' own assessment of their work.

And the schoolbooks? In an [article published in 2018](https://www.zinnedproject.org/if-we-knew-our-history/disguising-imperialism-textbooks-get-cold-war-wrong-dupe-students/), history teacher Ursula Wolfe-Rocca described the books used at her school. Writing for the Zinn Education Project, an advocacy outlet, she reported that Pearson's American Journey covered Iran and Guatemala but left out US meddling in Chile. The AP text, America: A Concise History, mentioned CIA-directed coups in Guatemala and Iran but had no reference to Chile's coup. This is one teacher's account of particular textbooks, not a survey of American knowledge or every edition. It establishes a specific omission, not its cause.

Wolfe-Rocca notes that the books' Cold War chapters stop before the Chilean coup, leaving later interventions scattered elsewhere or absent. That structure is a documented limitation. It does not prove anyone deliberately suppressed Allende. But a reader cannot assess an intervention that the book never describes. American Journey found room for the Shah and for United Fruit. A government elected at the ballot box is a strange thing to run out of space for.

The April memo describes newly enfranchised voters whom the opposition had underestimated. It also considers future funding to prevent Popular Unity from electing or imposing its presidential candidate in 1976. The next presidential election was already on the page. Beside it was a proposal for more money.
