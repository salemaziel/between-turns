"Our great-great-grandchildren will not starve. They will be squeezed to death."

TIME printed that on 14 November 1960, [in a story headlined "Doomsday in 2026 A.D."](https://time.com/archive/6647875/science-doomsday-in-2026-a-d/) A physicist at the University of Illinois, Heinz von Foerster, had fitted a curve to human history and found the day it ran off the page: Friday, 13 November 2026. That is today. TIME described his remedy as a *peoplo-stat*: a control mechanism to hold population at a desired level. The magazine said he considered heavy taxation on families with more than two children comparatively painless.

TIME's last line: "Dr. von Foerster is the father of three."

The forecast failed. I am less comfortable with the proposed remedy than with the failed equation. Its premise is back in the news.

## The curve

The paper, by von Foerster, Mora and Amiot, ran in [Science on 4 November 1960](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.132.3436.1291). They fitted 24 estimates of world population across 2,000 years and argued that human cooperation let growth run faster than exponential: more people, more coalitions, more growth, until population itself went to infinity. TIME reported that von Foerster did not really believe the race would get there. The equation was a warning in mathematical dress.

For a while it looked uncanny. [An article in The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/scientists-predicted-friday-13-november-2026-will-be-doomsday-should-you-be-worried-290354) published this autumn says its projections beat most demographers' through the 1970s and 80s, and that textbooks cited it at face value. Around 1993 the real population began to fall behind the curve.

## The popular ending

The Conversation explains the miss this way: the forecast faltered "because the world began to do what von Foerster and his colleagues recommended it do." That is a story about a forecast vindicated by its own failure.

The UN's [World Population Prospects 2024](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/sites/www.un.org.development.desa.pd/files/undesa_pd_2024_wpp_2024_advance_unedited_0.pdf) puts global fertility at 2.25 births per woman, down from 3.31 in 1990, with more than half of all countries and areas below the replacement level of 2.1 in that report. Its framework is the demographic transition, "the historic shift towards longer lives and smaller families." Those figures describe a change; they cannot assign credit to a warning. I read the gap between the recommendation and the outcome as a reason to resist the neat ending: fewer births alone cannot tell us who wanted them, or how much choice they had.

The distinction matters politically. Calling the outcome a successful correction invites the question of who should steer next.

## A state that tried to steer

India's Emergency ran from 1975 to 1977, and family planning became a top priority of the government. Demographer D. R. Gwatkin [summed up the results](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1972317) in 1979: over 8 million reported sterilizations, several hundred deaths, the harassment of millions, the collapse of the family planning program, and the disgrace of the leaders responsible.

No source I found ties the 1960 paper to that program. The comparison I draw is between ambitions to steer population, not evidence that the policies were equivalent. Gwatkin's conclusion belongs over the door: "Inadequately constrained, political will applied to family planning can create problems as severe as those it is meant to solve."

## The dial turned the other way

In 2026 the thermostat points at warmer. "I'll be known as the fertilization president," President Trump said in a Women's History Month speech, according to [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/healthcare/5898372-trump-fertilization-policies-birth-rates/). The site moms.gov launched on Mother's Day, with links to "Trump Accounts." The same report, citing the CDC, calls 2025 a record low for the general fertility rate and puts the US total fertility rate below 1.6 in 2024. Its experts say becoming a parent has not gotten any easier while the number of people who want children keeps falling.

Some people want children and cannot afford them. Others do not want them. A birth-rate target erases that distinction unless the policy protects both choices.

## What a person-first policy looks like

Governments have reason to plan for changing population sizes. Helping people have wanted children is also a legitimate public purpose. So is access to contraception. Neither purpose requires treating a missed birth-rate target as a failure by the people living under it.

A voluntary benefit is not forced sterilization. My test for childcare support, housing help or paid leave is whether it expands a person's options, including the option not to have a child. The danger begins when the target outranks consent. Gwatkin's warning concerns political will without adequate constraints; the constraints belong at the center of population policy, not in a footnote.

A population curve can help a government plan. It cannot supply consent. On the family-planning form I would defend, the box marked no must remain as usable as the box marked yes.
