The goodbye post names no individual author. Published on 5 November and signed only "Salon," it began: "Today, after 31 years, we are saddened to announce that Salon is ceasing publication." Then, on Bluesky, chief content officer Erin Keane [said](https://www.theverge.com/news/1028281/salon-dot-com-shuts-down) the editorial archive would "stay live for now."

Two words. A promise with the expiry printed on it.

By TheWrap's count Salon launched on [20 November 1995](https://www.thewrap.com/media-platforms/journalism/salon-shuts-down/), which makes today the 31st birthday of a publication that no longer publishes. The goodbye post is still readable as I review this. What "for now" is worth is a question the web already put to MTV News.

## The Monday links went to a homepage

MTV closed its news division in 2023. On Monday 24 June 2024, [Variety reported](https://variety.com/2024/digital/news/mtv-news-website-archives-pulled-offline-1236047163/), trying to open an MTV News article on mtvnews.com or mtv.com/news landed you on the main MTV site instead. Two decades of music journalism, including a hip-hop column, "Mixtape Mondays," that ran for nearly a decade, had stopped being addressable. Rolling Stone's Brian Hiatt noted that the older pieces did not turn up in the Wayback Machine either.

Paramount's answer, as Variety relayed it, was that all MTV News content was being preserved in a non-public archive. Variety's [follow-up](https://variety.com/2024/digital/news/mtv-news-articles-internet-archive-wayback-machine-1236058997/) added a line I keep rereading: a source familiar with the company said the sites drew very low traffic and cost more to run than they earned in ad revenue.

Preserved and readable are different promises. A reader whose link lands on a homepage cannot tell a drawer from a dumpster.

Michael Alex founded MTV News's digital operation and ran it from 1994 to 2007. A few days after the removal he wrote in Variety that the archive had vanished ["without warning, explanation or comment"](https://variety.com/2024/music/opinion/disappearance-of-mtv-news-online-archive-tragedy-1236052577/), and he gave the line I would put on the wall: "History needs stewards, not owners. Whoever legally owns the archive does not legally own the history."

## Salon's owner and the arithmetic

Since 2023 Salon has belonged to Find.co, which describes itself as a "Performance Marketing Ecosystem transforming crypto marketing with performance and precision." A marketing company now holds the back catalogue of a newsroom it just closed. Salon told the Associated Press it had ["no immediate plans"](https://www.oregonlive.com/business/2026/11/salon-pioneering-online-magazine-ceases-publication-after-three-decades.html) to take the archive down.

I read Paramount's reported arithmetic as a warning for other archives: an owner judging old journalism by advertising revenue can have a reason to stop serving it even while keeping the files. The web's baseline leans the same way: [Pew found](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2024/05/17/when-online-content-disappears/) that 38 percent of webpages that existed in 2013 were gone by October 2023, and that 23 percent of the news webpages it examined contained at least one broken link.

## The volunteer, and the hole in the volunteer

At MTV the work fell to the Internet Archive, which in the days after the takedown assembled a searchable index of 479,020 pages from mtv.com/news. Alex told Variety, "It's incomplete, but it's very impressive." Variety noted that the Wayback copy was not the full complement of what MTV had published.

Salon has the same backstop. On 12 November the Archive [announced](https://blog.archive.org/2026/11/12/preserving-30-years-of-salon-journalism/) that more than 250,000 Salon articles can be found in the Wayback Machine, and launched an "experimental" Salon Explorer to browse them. I am glad it exists. The Archive gives no total against which to check completeness. MTV shows why that gap matters: a search box can return thousands of results while articles and images remain missing.

## A copy with a public steward

Here is the rule I want: before a publication ceases or sells its archive, the owner must deposit a complete copy with a public steward: a national library, a university, or the Internet Archive. Full text, images, original URLs, public access. The owner keeps the copyright and any right to sell, license or train on it. What the owner gives up is the power to make the record unreadable.

The strongest objection is that public access restricts an asset even if copyright stays put. The steward also needs money to keep serving it. I would accept both costs rather than leave access contingent on the next owner's advertising calculation. A deposit rule needs funding for the receiving institution and an enforceable public-access license, not just a disk delivered to a library. The duty should begin at closure, while a handover can be part of the shutdown rather than a rescue attempted afterward.

## Three thousand three hundred and twenty-five

Variety reported that a Wayback search for "mixtape mondays" returned 3,325 results. A nonprofit built that search box in the days after a company switched off a site. To use it, the reader still needs the nonprofit to keep it running.
