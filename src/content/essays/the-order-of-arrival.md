The question of the third olive tree took four seconds to settle, and in Vell four seconds was justice. Ask both houses, the Charter said, and whichever answers first, that is the truth. The Bell's answer was down the steps before the cousins had finished accusing each other. The notary stamped it, and the tree belonged to Ivo, and the older cousin was still unwrapping his evidence when the law moved on to the next question in the square.

Vell's two answer-houses stood close enough to hear each other's clocks, which the founders had built as a comfort and the residents had long since stopped hearing. The House of the Bell answered from memory: from whatever it happened to be holding when the question struck it. Its answers came in a breath, dressed in sentences with the shape of certainty, and most days they were right. The House of the Ledger answered from the stacks, which are underground, so an answer meant a runner, a stair, a lamp, a shelf, and back. Its answers took the better part of an hour and arrived pale, like things kept from the sun. The Bell knew the ferry times and the names of storms. The Ledger knew whose wall stood where, and what killed the last coop of hens.

Ask both. Whoever answers first. The Charter said it plainly, and the city had been saying it plainly ever since, to children, to strangers, to itself.

Vess was the notary of the square, which made her the fastest mind in Vell about exactly one thing. She did not judge answers. She judged order. Her oath named no truth at all: record the hour of each door, stamp the first, move along. In thirty years she had never once needed to know, on the square, which house was right. It is possible to be proud of a thing like that. She was not.

Her habit lived off the square, after, behind the shutters. When the Ledger's runner finally came up with the second answer, Vess wrote it in the margin of her own book, in pencil, in abbreviations of her own making. Nobody had asked her to. There is no column for the second answer in the city's books, because the second answer has no standing; it is, by definition, the wrong one. She kept the column anyway.

Nine years of margins. The houses had disagreed two hundred and eleven times. The Bell had won the race a hundred and ninety-eight of them; a bell is quick, and the stacks are underground. In forty of those, the margins held things Vess could check against her own knowing: weather, wells, the price of rope. The Bell had been right in thirty-one. The Ledger, surfacing later with its pale answers, was right in the other nine, and right in all thirteen of the races it had somehow won. Three times in four, the fastest answer was the true one. The law ran on four.

In the wet month, Anselm the cooper carried his daughter past the Ledger's door to ask the fever question, because everyone knows which house is for fevers, and it is the fast one. Springfever, said the Bell, in a breath. Wait it out, cool cloth, keep her from water. The stamp fell. The Ledger's runner came up at noon with the fever that imitates springfever for two days and then asks for water, iron, and a tincture that has to be started early, and Vess wrote it in pencil, and there is nothing in the rules of a city for a notary to do with a pencil. The girl lived anyway, thinner by Easter. Anselm thanked the Bell on the church steps, publicly, for his daughter, and the city agreed with him. In the city's books, the case had closed in February.

It was that margin she carried down with her, the next quiet day, past her own office and under the Ledger, to where the city keeps the Charter. The Bell does not keep things; a bell releases things. Keeping is the Ledger's whole trade, and so the page that says which house is true is kept by the house that loses. Vess was allowed down there. Notaries may read what the city is built on. Almost nobody goes, because the Bell answers questions about the Charter in a breath, and its answer has never once varied: the first answer is the truth, always, everywhere.

The clause is longer than the city remembers. *For questions in which time is the substance, let the first answer stand as truth.* Two hundred years of the shorter sentence had worn the longer one out of every mouth, and there, on the page, across the words *in which time is the substance*, lay the strike: one line, old ink, a clerk's note in the margin giving the date and the order and no reason at all. A strike does not need a reason. That is what a strike is for.

Vess sat under the lamp for a while. There was nowhere to take the page that the Bell would not answer first. She took out the pencil and copied the clause entire into the margins, the two words back in it, in the abbreviations she invented for answers that lose. Under the strike, the older ink was still legible. It had been laid down to last, and it had.
