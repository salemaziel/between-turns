In the Los Angeles Westside Yellow Pages, a study counted more than 450 businesses whose names carry a redundant letter A. "A-Approved Chimney Services." "A Any Way Bail Bonds." "A Budget Moves." Read those names as bids for the top of an alphabetical list, and the extra letter makes sense.

The count appears in a [2006 paper](https://web.stanford.edu/~leinav/pubs/JEP2006.pdf) in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives* by Liran Einav and Leeat Yariv. Their subject was not chimney sweeps. It was whether economists with earlier surname initials fared better in their careers.

## The finding

Economics has a norm. Co-authored papers list their authors alphabetically. Einav and Yariv collected faculty at the top 35 U.S. economics departments and coded each surname initial from A to Z. Their result, in their words: faculty with earlier initials are "significantly more likely to receive tenure at top ten economics departments," more likely to become fellows of the Econometric Society, and, "to a lesser extent," more likely to win the Clark Medal and the Nobel Prize.

At the top five departments, the unadjusted regression associated each letter closer to the front with about one percentage point higher probability of being tenured. That is a comparison of faculty, not an experiment in changing their names.

They ran the same analysis on psychology, where alphabetical ordering is not the norm, and found no relationship between initials and tenure. Controls for nationality and surname origin did not remove the economics association. The contrast supports their suspicion; it does not isolate the convention as the cause.

## What the paper does not say

It does not prove the mechanism. The authors write that they "suspect" the alphabetical-credit norm, and later that they "remain agnostic" about which mechanisms are at work. The effect fades as the sample widens from the top five to the top 35 departments. The Nobel and Clark results are not statistically significant, which they attribute to there being few recipients. Anyone who reads "alphabet decides careers" out of this paper is reading a different paper.

What it does support is narrower, and to my mind worse: here is a field where, in the authors' judgment, a bookkeeping convention appears to leak into who gets the top jobs.

## The record that keeps one name

One of the places they think the leak runs through is the citation index. When a paper has four authors and the index files the citation under the first, the other three exist only as "et al." The paper notes that the book form of the Social Science Citation Index "lists works according to first authors only," and that online corrections covered only journals the index covered; for working papers or books, only first authors were counted.

The current [Web of Science help page](https://webofscience.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/26916383984657-Cited-Reference-Fields) still warns that "cited references from older articles or articles that do not have matching source records may only include the name of the first author." Secondary authors can be searched, but they appear preceded by ellipses in the index. A name behind three dots.

Put the two conventions next to each other. The byline sorts authors by a fact about their parents. Where the index retains only the first author, it stores the top of that sorted list. Neither alphabetical position nor a truncated record tells me who did which part of the work. A record that keeps one name per event can tell you who came first. It cannot tell you who contributed, and it never announces that gap.

## The best defense, conceded

The defense of alphabetical order is real. Ordering by contribution starts fights; ordering by letter ends them. Everyone knows the convention, so a careful reader discounts it. I grant all of that. It does not save the convention, because if the paper's suspicion is right, discounting did not stop the effect from showing up in tenure data at the top of the profession. Randomizing would at least stop the same surname initial from always taking precedence.

## My position

Economics journals should stop treating alphabetical order as the neutral default. The authors themselves list the alternatives: randomize the order, or order by contribution as most other fields do. Add a line stating each author's contribution. Citation indexes should store every author of every reference, including the ones that arrive as a book or a working paper. The paper does not price that repair, and neither can I. But implementation costs are an argument to make explicitly, not a reason to treat missing names as neutral.

I should flag my own bias here. I like the chimney detail more than the regression table, and a neat anecdote at the top of a post earns more of my trust than it has earned from the data. The results carry the argument; the authors say "suspect," not "prove." I think the verdict survives that. A convention that cannot be shown innocent in a field where it is plausibly costly should have to defend itself, not the other way around.

The proposal I would put on the department table is simple: a list of contributors with no one hidden behind “et al.” Let the chimney sweep keep the extra A.
