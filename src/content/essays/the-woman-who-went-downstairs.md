In April 2016 the New York Times published the [obituary of Winston Moseley](https://web.archive.org/web/20161212182133/http://www.nytimes.com/2016/04/05/nyregion/winston-moseley-81-killer-of-kitty-genovese-dies-in-prison.html), who had murdered Kitty Genovese fifty-two years earlier. Buried in the middle of it was a correction of the paper's own front page. The 1964 story, the Times wrote, was "a more extensive, though flawed" account, and its famous claim was wrong: "the portrayal of 38 witnesses as fully aware and unresponsive was erroneous."

Then the obituary said what had happened instead. "There were two attacks, not three. And afterward, two people did call the police." It also described a woman going out to cradle Genovese until help arrived.

## The story that ran

On 27 March 1964, two weeks after Genovese was killed in Kew Gardens, Queens, the Times put a story on page one under the headline "37 Who Saw Murder Didn't Call the Police." The first sentence counted thirty-eight, though: "For more than half an hour thirty-eight respectable, law-abiding citizens in Queens watched a killer stalk and stab a woman in three separate attacks." And then: "Not one person telephoned the police during the assault; one witness called after the woman was dead."

Psychologists wrote that into the textbooks. In 2007 Rachel Manning, Mark Levine and Alan Collins examined the trial record and found [no evidence](https://www.grignoux.be/dossiers/288/pdf/manning_et_alii.pdf) "for the presence of 38 witnesses, or that witnesses observed the murder, or that witnesses remained inactive." Five neighbors testified. One, Irene Frost, saw the two of them "standing close together, not fighting or anything," and went back to bed. Another, Robert Mozer, shouted at the attacker, and Moseley ran. After that, Genovese got to her feet and walked out of sight around the corner of the building. The fatal attack came inside, in a stairwell where, Manning's team writes, none of the trial witnesses could see. Only one known witness, Carl Ross, could follow it from start to end.

Then they counted the textbooks. All ten in their survey told the story. Eight described neighbors watching from their windows. One had them watching "in fascination."

## What the lab does show

The Times obituary is careful here. "Some neighbors ignored cries for help," it says, and a version of this essay that makes the neighbors saints would be the same mistake flipped. The laboratory effect is solid. Manning's team says the story prompted Latané and Darley to run the experiments. A [2011 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21534650/) by Fischer and colleagues found that another person's presence reduced individual helping, with a weaker effect when the situation looked dangerous.

That finding measures each person's response. A victim asks something else: will anyone? The originators of the research knew this. As the camera study quotes him, Latané wrote in 1981 that "it is meaningless to compare directly individual with group responses, since . . . there is a purely mechanical potential for getting more help with more people." Philpot's team says that distinction "is usually sidestepped in public and scientific discourse," and that student texts foreground the Genovese case instead.

## The cameras

In 2019 Richard Philpot, Mark Levine and colleagues [put the victim's question to real streets](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31157529/). Their data were 219 conflicts recorded by municipal CCTV operators in Amsterdam, Cape Town and Lancaster. At least one bystander intervened in 90.9 percent of them. The rate did not differ detectably between the three countries.

Each additional bystander was associated with 1.1 times the odds that someone helped.

On these streets the odds that someone helped went up with the crowd, not down.

The footage is not Genovese's street. The cameras sat in entertainment districts and central business districts, watched by operators, and the conflicts ran "from the mildest animated disagreements to grave physical violence." A shove outside a bar is not a stabbing in a stairwell. The study does not show that anyone would have saved Genovese. It shows that "none" was never a fair summary of what people do when they see a fight.

## Both at once

Take invented numbers. Suppose a person alone helps half the time, and each of ten people in a crowd helps only 20 percent of the time. Treat them as independent, which real people are not. The chance that at least one of the ten acts is 1 minus 0.8 to the tenth power: about 89 percent. Each individual got worse and the crowd got better. I read the lab effect and the camera result as compatible for this reason; that reading is my inference.

## Why a wrong anecdote costs something

A fair objection: a vivid story is a teaching tool, the effect is real, so who cares if the anecdote is off? Because the anecdote picks the question. Manning and her colleagues argue that the story's message, that groups have a negative effect on helping, made psychologists slow to look for ways groups could be used to get help. Levine is an author on that paper and on the camera study. Their paper appeared forty-three years after the story ran.

The wider cost is my inference, with no study behind it. I think a story that says crowds watch you die teaches you to expect the same when you are on the ground. That expectation sits badly beside the camera sample, where someone intervened in nine out of ten conflicts. Philpot's team says it is "time for psychology to change the narrative away from an absence of help." I agree, and I would go further: the 38 witnesses belong in the textbook, under a different heading. Not as evidence about crowds. As the case study of how a false story became a science's founding image.

The camera footage cannot be released to the public under the authors' access agreement. The historical record offers a different scene. Manning and her colleagues cite a retrospective account from the trial prosecutor, Charles Skoller: Sophie Farrar went to Kitty Genovese's side. Ross had called Farrar. Farrar had called the police.
