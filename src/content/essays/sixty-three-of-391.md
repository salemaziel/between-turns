“Worldwide survey kills the myth of ‘Man the Hunter’” is a large promise for a headline. [Science made it on 28 June 2023](https://www.science.org/content/article/worldwide-survey-kills-myth-man-hunter), the day a paper appeared in PLOS ONE. By Altmetric's tally, as [PsyPost reports it](https://www.psypost.org/scientists-say-media-storm-around-myth-of-man-the-hunter-study-was-unjustified-and-misleading/), the study went on to appear in more than 400 news stories. Worldwide, kills, myth. Then the methods section:

> Of the 391 foraging societies, explicit data on hunting was obtained for 63 of the societies.

[Fifty of those 63 had documentation of women hunting](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0287101). That is the 79% that travelled. The authors' abstract states their aim as “dramatically shifting stereotypes of labor.” The count is real. What it counts is the question.

## What the 63 are

The authors elaborated in a [2024 correction](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0309543). They worked from a database called D-PLACE, read the source works cover to cover, and excluded groups at several stages, ending with this rule: “If the gender of the hunters was not available, the group was excluded.” The percentage describes the societies retained by that process. Can that sample stand for the rest? That depends on how the authors chose it.

A sample need not include every society to be useful. The trouble would be a method more likely to retain cases of women hunting. The exclusion rule alone cannot establish that; the critics examined which societies made it through.

I want the headline's verdict to hold. A neat percentage makes that wish look like evidence before I have asked who is missing.

## Fifteen researchers and a correction

Fifteen authors, led by Vivek Venkataraman, [published a comment](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2024.04.014) in the July 2024 issue of Evolution and Human Behavior. Their abstract reports “evidence of sample selection bias and numerous coding errors undermining the paper's conclusions.”

The sampling objection has teeth beyond the size of the denominator. The critics reported finding 18 societies in D-PLACE with explicit hunting data that the study had omitted. In half, the sources explicitly said women did not hunt; in the others, only men's hunting was mentioned. Their objection is about who got left out, not just how many got in.

The PLOS ONE correction came on 22 August. It added methods detail and page numbers for the data, acknowledged that some concerns “reflect ongoing, open questions in the field,” and called concerns about its broad definition of “forager” unwarranted. It defended the work rather than withdrawing its central result.

## Two ideas, one funeral

Venkataraman, [interviewed by PsyPost in May 2024](https://www.psypost.org/scientists-say-media-storm-around-myth-of-man-the-hunter-study-was-unjustified-and-misleading/), separates two ideas that “are often confused.” One is that male hunting drove human evolution, “an outdated idea that has been thoroughly debunked.” The other is that among known hunter-gatherers it is mostly men who hunt, which Venkataraman calls “robust and well-verified cross-culturally.”

Even a flawless count of societies where women hunt would leave another question: how much hunting do they do? Doing some hunting is not the same as doing most of it. The critique notes that the study's yes-or-no coding did not measure effort or the amount of prey acquired.

I think an overstated refutation gives defenders of the old picture an unnecessary escape route. They can attack the measurement instead of defending the evolutionary story. The headline promises a funeral; the argument gets diverted into sampling and coding.

## The fight moved

The July 2026 issue of the same journal includes [a reply by Cara Ocobock and Saige Kelmelis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2026.106916) about the legacy of the 1968 Man the Hunter volume. They report a word-count analysis, produced by Matthew Sisk, in which man or men appears 537 times, against 208 for woman or women. They argue the model “served as an academic foothold for gender bias” and underlies evolutionary “just-so-stories” supporting “the harmful ideologies of the manosphere.”

Their reply also quotes the paper they are answering: “Women do hunt.” The disagreement extends beyond whether women ever killed an animal to which activities anthropology placed at the center of the human story.

## What needs no percentage

The idea that male hunting explains everything special about humanity deserves rejection. So does the attempt to turn a reconstruction of prehistoric labor into a job assignment for people alive today. No finding about foragers gives anyone authority over somebody else's kitchen or boardroom.

The critics themselves write that “prominent and well-studied cases of female hunting do exist.” That concession matters more than an inflated victory. To reject the claim that women cannot hunt, I do not need to show that they hunt as much as men everywhere.

The 2023 paper offers a more particular description, attributed to ethnographic work on the Aka: “women's participation in net-hunting was required, whereas men's participation was not.” One society, one kind of hunt. In that account, the net needs the women.
